Chapter I · Manufacturing Chance

From flat uniform bits to the Gaussian bell curve

A digital computer is deterministic. Its pseudo-random number generator (like the Linear Congruential Generator in prng.py) only produces flat, uniform numbers between 0 and 1. How do we turn flat coin-flips into the bell-shaped shocks of a financial market?

1. The Mathematical Transform

George Box and Mervin Muller (1958) solved this geometrically. Take two independent uniform draws U_1, U_2 \in (0,1). Use U_2 to spin a compass needle to angle \theta = 2\pi U_2, and use U_1 to stretch the radius by R = \sqrt{-2\ln U_1}. The Cartesian shadow Z_1 = R\cos\theta is an exact standard normal draw \mathcal{N}(0,1).

2. Try It on the Canvas (←)

Drag the U_1 slider toward 0.005 while watching the rotating vector on the left. Because -\ln(U_1) explodes as U_1 \to 0, the radius shoots outward and drops your shock into the extreme 3\sigma tail of the bell curve.

3. How Trading Desks Use This

Black Monday and the 2008 crash live in that tiny corner where U_1 \to 0. Because pure random sampling can miss rare corners in small samples, quantitative desks often feed Quasi-Monte Carlo Sobol sequences into the Box–Muller transform so the unit square is covered evenly without gaps.

Chapter II · Itô's Lemma & Volatility Drag

Why Brownian jaggedness pulls the median below the mean

Once we have a Gaussian shock Z, how does it move the stock price? Stock prices compound multiplicatively so they never go negative: dS_t = r S_t\,dt + \sigma S_t\,dW_t. Solving that differential equation reveals the most famous surprise in stochastic calculus.

1. Why Ordinary Calculus Fails

In freshman calculus, small squared changes (dt)^2 vanish. But a Brownian path W_t scales with \sqrt{t}, meaning its squared step is (dW_t)^2 = dt. When we apply a Taylor expansion to \ln S_t, Itô's Lemma forces us to keep the second derivative term -\tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^2 dt.

2. Try It on the Canvas (←)

Keep Z = 0 and drag volatility \sigma up to 60%. Watch the solid ember curve (Itô's exact median path) sink far below the white mean curve S_0 e^{rT}. Intuitively: a +50% gain followed by a −50% loss leaves $100 at $75, not $100. Volatility taxes compounding.

3. In the Engine & Real Markets

Because Itô's solution is exact over any horizon T, gbm.py jumps directly from t=0 to expiry in one step with zero discretization bias. In real markets, this -\tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^2 term explains why 3× leveraged ETFs bleed value in volatile, sideways markets.

Chapter III · Risk-Neutral Valuation

Where the hockey-stick payoff meets the lognormal cloud

A European call option gives you the right, but not the obligation, to buy the stock at strike K at expiry T. Its terminal payoff is the hockey stick f(S_T) = \max(S_T - K, 0). How do we price that uncertain future payout today?

1. Deconstructing Black–Scholes

The fair price is the discounted integral of the payoff times the probability density: e^{-rT}\int f(S)p(S)\,dS. Black and Scholes proved this integral splits cleanly into S_0 N(d_1) - K e^{-rT} N(d_2). Here N(d_2) is the exact probability of finishing in the money, and N(d_1) weights the shares you receive by how deep in the money they land.

2. Try It on the Canvas (←)

Watch the animated probability particles fall onto the density curve on the left. Slide the Strike K right and left: the top shaded area is N(d_2), and the bottom ember area is the expected payoff that Monte Carlo estimates by averaging random darts.

3. Why Real Stock Drift Disappears

Notice that the stock's true bull/bear drift \mu is completely absent! Because a dealer can hold \Delta = N(d_1) shares against each option sold, directional risk cancels out. By no-arbitrage, a hedged portfolio must grow at the risk-free rate r.

Chapter IV · Antithetic Variates

Breaking the 1/\sqrt{N} speed limit with mirror symmetry

By the Central Limit Theorem, Monte Carlo error shrinks as \sigma_{\text{payoff}} / \sqrt{N}. Cutting error by 10× takes 100× more paths. Instead of burning 100× more CPU cycles, we can use mathematical symmetry to shrink the numerator.

1. Negative Covariance Is Free Precision

Since the standard normal bell curve is symmetric around zero, every shock +Z has an equally probable twin -Z. When +Z sends the stock soaring into a huge payoff f^+, its mirror -Z sends the twin path down into a small or zero payoff f^-. Their errors cancel when averaged: \bar{f} = \tfrac{1}{2}(f^+ + f^-).

2. Try It on the Canvas (←)

Drag the +Z slider back and forth. Watch the ember path (+Z) and frost path (-Z) swing wildly in opposite directions while their midpoint payoff \bar{f} (pulsing in the center) stays remarkably close to the exact Black–Scholes price.

3. Exotic Options on Wall Street

For path-dependent contracts (Asian options, barrier knock-outs, autocallables), no closed-form Black–Scholes formula exists—Monte Carlo is the only way to price them. Variance reduction (variance_reduction.py) cuts compute time in half during live market hours.

Why Delta is both a slope and a hedge ratio

On the top chart, the solid ember curve is Black–Scholes value V(S) and the straight paper line is its tangent of slope Δ. When a dealer sells you an option and buys Δ shares, their hedged portfolio rides that tangent line: small spot moves cancel out, while large shocks leave only second-order Gamma curvature.
